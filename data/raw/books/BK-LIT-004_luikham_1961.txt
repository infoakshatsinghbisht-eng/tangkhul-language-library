AUTHOR'S FOREWORD (T. LUIKHAM, 1961)
Our ancient laws (Mayonza) were preserved without written script through the unbending memory of our elders. With the rapid spread of modern courts, there is grave peril that our ancestral rights, land boundaries, and moral values will be misinterpreted. This work records the exact precedents, judicial procedures, and compensatory scales of the Wung (Tangkhul) people. — T. Luikham.

CHAPTER 1: THE JURISPRUDENTIAL PHILOSOPHY OF WUNG LAW
Wung (Tangkhul) customary law does not distinguish rigidly between civil tort and criminal injury; rather, every offense is understood as a breach of cosmic and communal equilibrium (shak-kasa). The primary goal of judicial proceedings is not punitive incarceration, but the restitution of social harmony through compensatory settlement.
Law is rooted in three principles: (1) Respect for Ancestral Covenant (Khararchanwui Tuingashit), (2) Preservation of Clan Integrity (Shangwui Kaphung), and (3) Absolute Truthfulness before Varivara.

--- Page 5 ---
CHAPTER 1 (Continued): THE SOVEREIGN JURISDICTION OF THE VILLAGE
Every Wung village possesses sovereign jurisdiction over all acts committed within its boundary markers (ram-chui). An offense committed within the village gates is adjudicated by the Hangva, regardless of whether the offender is a native villager or a guest.
Inter-village injuries are referred to joint tribunals presided over by neutral Awungas and Pukreila intermediaries.

--- Page 6 ---
CHAPTER 1 (Continued): STATUS OF PERSONS UNDER CUSTOMARY LAW
Legal personhood is anchored within the clan. An individual without clan affiliation possesses no standing before the council. The clan stands surety for the civil and moral debts of its members. If a clansman commits an offense and is unable to pay the prescribed compensation (shiman), the liability falls collectively upon his immediate lineage.

CHAPTER 2: MARRIAGE LAWS (NGALONG KALA MAYONZA)
Marriage is an alliance between two distinct clans (shang). Strict exogamy is enforced. Marriage with a maternal aunt's daughter or mother's clan was preferred (cross-cousin marriage), cementing alliances across alternate generations.
The prospective groom presents formal gifts of rice-beer (kham), cloth, and iron implements to the bride's father during the betrothal negotiations (tui-kachathei).

--- Page 8 ---
CHAPTER 2 (Continued): BRIDE WEALTH (SHIMAN) AND DOWRY
Bride wealth among the Tangkhul is not a commercial transaction, but a symbolic recompense to the bride's clan for the loss of her productive and reproductive labor. Typical marriage gifts include cattle (se), brass gong (sen-kuir), and handwoven shawls.
In return, the bride enters the marital household with dowry goods (ngalawui phunglung): grain baskets, loom apparatus, bead necklaces, and domestic brass vessels.

--- Page 9 ---
CHAPTER 2 (Continued): DISSOLUTION OF MARRIAGE AND SEPARATION
Divorce was uncommon in traditional society and subject to rigorous financial penalties. If a husband dismissed his wife without legitimate customary fault (such as adultery or witchcraft), he forfeited all bride wealth and was required to provide her with subsistence grain (shang-kham).
If a wife deserted her husband, her natal clan was obliged to return the entirety of the marriage gifts received.

CHAPTER 3: CLAN INHERITANCE (KASHAK) AND LAND TENURES
Terrace land (Luingam) is held in perpetuity by families and lineages. Under no circumstance may ancestral land be sold to an outsider from another village. If an individual is compelled to alienate land due to debt or famine, he must first offer the right of preemption to his nearest clan brethren (shangwui ngacham-mi).
Communal hill tracts and fuel reserves belong to the village as a whole under the stewardship of the Awunga.

--- Page 11 ---
CHAPTER 3 (Continued): INHERITANCE BY PRIMOGENITURE
The eldest son (kaphung-mayar) inherits the ancestral home (shim-kharar) and the principal terrace fields. In return, he assumes the sacred obligation of maintaining aging parents, performing funeral ceremonies (thikahaiwui ot), and hosting clan elders during festivals.
Younger sons receive secondary plots of land and are assisted by the lineage in felling timber and building their own homesteads upon marriage.

CHAPTER 4: CIVIL WRONGS, THEFTS, AND RESTITUTION (SHIMAN)
Theft of domestic animals (pigs, cows, buffaloes) was punished by threefold restitution (thum-ngashit): returning the stolen animal plus two additional animals of equal value as fine (kashiman) to the aggrieved family, plus a cleansing pig to the village council for feast consumption.
Theft of harvested grain from field granaries was regarded as an aggravated offense against the entire agricultural community, requiring immediate public apology and heavy indemnity.

--- Page 13 ---
CHAPTER 4 (Continued): PHYSICAL INJURIES AND BLOOD COMPENSATION
Accidental physical injury was compensated according to the severity of the wound and loss of working days. The responsible party was required to provide rice, fowl, and medicinal herbs during the convalescence of the victim.
Homicide, even if accidental, resulted in the temporary banishment of the slayer from the village to prevent immediate blood retaliation (rai-shiman), followed by protracted peace negotiations mediated by the Pukreila.

CHAPTER 5: JUDICIAL OATHS AND TRIAL BY ORDEAL (THEISHAK)
When oral testimony conflicted and evidence was inconclusive, the Hangva administered judicial oaths (theishak). An oath was not taken lightly; it was believed that invoking false testimony in the name of Varivara, the earth, or the clan ancestors would result in premature death, blindness, or the extinction of the swearer's lineage.
The most solemn oath involved biting a tiger's tooth (shilwui ha) or holding a spear point to the throat.

--- Page 15 ---
CHAPTER 5 (Continued): WATER DIVINATION AND ORDEALS
In property boundary disputes, contestants submitted to water immersion ordeals (tara-kachung). Both parties dove simultaneously into a designated river pool; the individual who surfaced first was adjudged the dishonest party.
Alternatively, hot water tests (tara khalang-li pang khamayei) were conducted under the scrutiny of the village priest.

CHAPTER 6: TRADITIONAL TABOOS (KHAMTHEI) AND SANCTUARY
Certain days were declared Khamthei (sacred rest days / genna) during which all agricultural labor, weaving, and woodchopping were forbidden. Breaching Khamthei was believed to bring blight upon crops and attracted village fines.
The homestead of the Awunga served as a sanctuary. A fugitive fleeing vengeance who succeeded in touching the Awunga's central house-pillar (shim-chui) was granted temporary immunity while elders convened to arbitrate.

--- Page 17 ---
CHAPTER 6 (Continued): PURIFICATION OF HEARTHS
Following unnatural deaths (lightning strikes, falls from cliffs, childbirth complications), the household was declared polluted (khamatei). Sacred cleansing ceremonies (shim-kashak) were conducted by the village Maiba before normal social intercourse and commensality could be resumed with neighboring families.

--- Page 18 ---
CHAPTER 7: VILLAGE ADMINISTRATION AND THE AWUNGA'S OFFICE
The Awunga was endowed with customary prerogatives: he received the right thigh of all game felled in village forests, was entitled to communal labor on his fields during the sowing and harvest seasons, and received a portion of all adjudication fines.
In exchange, the Awunga was obligated to display unfailing hospitality, maintain the village skull depository and covenant stones, and represent the community in all external treaties.

--- Page 19 ---
CHAPTER 7 (Continued): CODIFICATION AND THE MODERN LEGAL REALITY
Under the Manipur Hill Peoples Regulation (1947) and subsequent district council legislation, Tangkhul customary courts were granted formal statutory recognition to adjudicate civil and customary disputes.
The preservation of these time-tested norms ensures that justice remains speedy, cost-free, restorative, and deeply rooted in the cultural ethos of the Tangkhul people.

LEGAL GLOSSARY & TRADITIONAL MAXIMS
Key jurisprudence terms in Luikham (1961):
- Mayonza: Customary law, code of ethics, justice system.
- Hangva: Council of elders, village judicial bench.
- Shiman: Restitution, compensatory fine, indemnity.
- Theishak: Solemn judicial oath, trial by ordeal.
- Luingam: Terrace field tenure, private agricultural property.
- Pukreila: Sacred female ambassador, peacemaker between combatant villages.
