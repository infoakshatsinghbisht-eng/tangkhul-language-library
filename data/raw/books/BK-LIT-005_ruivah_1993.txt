PREFACE (PROF. KHASHIM RUIVAH, 1993)
The soul of the Tangkhul people resides in their folk ballads (Haola). In traditional village life, every major occasion—the turning of the sod at seed-sowing, the weeding of the terraces, the courter's dialogue at the girls' dormitory, and the final lament over the deceased—was given musical and poetic expression. This volume presents authentic field transcriptions with critical apparatus and English translations. Imphal, 1993.

CHAPTER 1: EPIC BALLADS OF ANCESTRAL HEROES
The ancestral epic of Luikham and Raikham commemorates the legendary founders who cleared the ancient forests of the eastern hill ranges. Sung during major communal festivals, the ballad recounts their archery contests, the felling of the great timber at Shirui, and the establishment of peaceful boundaries between hill clans.
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Shirui kashongli kashung thot,
   Luikham mayarna thing-chui sathei;
   Shangwui khonkhur kachiva ngasoda,
   Wungram khararchan mathan salu!'

--- Page 5 ---
CHAPTER 1 (Continued): ENGLISH RENDERING AND ANALYSIS
English Translation:
  'On the high ridge of Shirui Peak,
   Hero Luikham felled the sacred tree;
   All the clans gathered with one voice,
   Singing the praise of the ancestral land!'
The metre follows the traditional heptasyllabic cadence (seven syllables per line) typical of classical Tangkhul folk poetry, with internal rhyme and paired metaphorical couplets (tui-ngatei).

CHAPTER 1 (Continued): THE VOYAGE TO THE EASTERN VALLEYS
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Khanggo shongli zattang thotna,
   Khayeikatha tara khorli;
   Meithei ramli ngaso-chithei,
   Raikham mayarna phara pamsai.'
Translation: As they journeyed toward the eastern valleys, beside the sparkling clear streams, brother Raikham established peaceful commerce with the peoples of the plains, cementing ancestral brotherhood.

CHAPTER 2: AGRICULTURAL CHANTS OF LUIRA PHANIT
Luira Phanit is the principal agricultural festival celebrated in February. The entire village assembles at the Awunga's courtyard to sing the seed-sowing chant (Hao La) invoking the blessings of the earth and heavens upon the approaching season.
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Luira phanit rarang thotna,
   Luingam ngaleili raruikatha chitheilu;
   Varivara kaphungli khangamor,
   Zingkum hili rui-kashang phanlu!'

--- Page 8 ---
CHAPTER 2 (Continued): THE WEEDING SONG (LUI-KAPHIT LA)
During the arduous monsoon weeding season, communal labor groups (ngashun) sing rhythmic work songs to coordinate hoeing movements along the steep terraces:
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Phaphuk ngashingda lui-kaphitli,
   Ngala-mayar ngasoda vangasai;
   Thangkhamir kachangli khangarao,
   Kong-khor tara mangda ngasolu!'

--- Page 9 ---
CHAPTER 2 (Continued): HARVEST ANTHEM (CHUMPHA LA)
Chumpha marks the joyous storage of grain in December:
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Chumpha phanit kasang thotna,
   Raizan shimli kashung kaping;
   Ava-ayi shiman mathailakla,
   Miwung kachivali tingkhami phanlu!'
Translation: When the Chumpha feast arrives, granaries are filled to overflowing; let no household suffer want, and may prosperity dwell with all the people.

CHAPTER 3: ROMANTIC COURTING DIALOGUES (MAWONZAI)
At the Ngala Longshim (girls' dormitory), suitors visited in the evening to engage in poetic duets. Courtship was conducted through intricate metaphorical riddles, where direct speech was avoided in favor of floral and bird allegories.
The Suitor's Verse (Mayarwui La):
  'Kazing kashungli won-kapha,
   Ngalawui maiwon theisai;
   Iwui malungli khayangna,
   Nali kongashi phunglikha.'

--- Page 11 ---
CHAPTER 3 (Continued): THE MAIDEN'S RESPONSE (NGALAWUI NGAHAN)
The Maiden's Response:
  'Shirui wonna kachang phungsai,
   Kha mayar nawui tuichi kashung;
   Shangwui khararbing ngashon-khalakha,
   Iwui malungli tingkhami phanlu.'
Translation: The Shirui Lily blooms on the mountain, yet the young man's words ring true; if the elders of the clans grant their blessing, peace shall find a home within my heart.

CHAPTER 4: HUNTING INCANTATIONS (KHAMASUNG LA)
Hunting was a sacred pursuit requiring spiritual preparation and taboos. Before entering virgin forest tracts, the chief hunter sang incantations to appease the forest spirits (Khara-kameo):
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Ngashan phungli kapei khangaran,
   Khara-kameoli dori khami;
   Shi-wung kachangli khanasung,
   Awungali share khayao salu!'

--- Page 13 ---
CHAPTER 4 (Continued): RETURN OF THE SUCCESSFUL HUNTER
Upon felling wild game (stag, boar, serow), the hunting party sounded the victory horn (tala-khon) and entered the village gates singing triumphant chants. The right thigh was delivered to the Awunga, and the skull mounted on the hunter's portico as a mark of merit.

CHAPTER 4 (Continued): THE SEROW CHANT (SAYAT LA)
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Kaphung kachangli kashung sayat,
   Haophi kachonli mathan theikahai;
   Pangmashan mayarna theisatkatha,
   Longshimli la kashak phanlu!'
Translation: The serow roaming high cliffs has been struck down; let the dormitory youth gather and raise the hunting song of triumph!

CHAPTER 5: FUNERAL ELEGIES AND LAMENTS (KATHI LA)
Tangkhul funeral poetry expresses profound grief while guiding the departed spirit (kathi mangan) along the perilous trail to Kazeiram (the underworld realm of spirits):
Tangkhul Verse:
  'I-va o, nawui zatkatha shongfali,
   Kazeiram kongli kaphan thotna;
   Khamatei ngathumli macharanlu,
   Khararbingna nali khaphung phungsai!'

--- Page 16 ---
CHAPTER 5 (Continued): FAREWELL TO THE DEPARTED SOUL
Translation:
  'O beloved father, along the path where you journey,
   When you reach the dark river of Kazeiram;
   Do not fear the shadow of the unknown,
   For the ancestors stand ready to welcome your soul!'
Mourners beat the wooden slit-log drum (khamor-thing) in slow, syncopated rhythms accompanying the funeral chant.

CHAPTER 6: MARTIAL ANTHEMS AND VICTORY BALLADS (RAI LA)
Martial anthems were sung by warriors departing for battle and upon returning from expeditions. These songs invoked the courage of lineage heroes and celebrated the defense of the village ramparts:
Tangkhul Verse:
  'Wungram kashangli kachisobing,
   Kachu-kaphi pangli phungda vangasai;
   Rari-kapangna raranglikha,
   Ithumwui ramli mazanglak mana!'

--- Page 18 ---
CHAPTER 6 (Continued): THE PEACEMAKING FINALE
Following conflict, warriors assembled with their adversaries under the guidance of the Pukreila to sing the solemn reconciliation hymn, burying spearheads in the ground and swearing eternal non-aggression.

CHAPTER 7: PROVERBS AND MORAL ALLEGORIES IN SONG
Folk singers embedded tribal philosophy into short, memorable lyrical proverbs (Paotam La) taught to children around winter fires:
- 'Khamatei sachitheikha maram kashung mathailak mana' (A twisted beginning cannot yield an upright crop).
- 'Shangwui tuili ngashon-katha kazing kashungli pamsai' (He who heeds clan counsel walks under clear skies).
- 'Luingamli mik kachangna raizanli raruikatha theilikha' (Eyes that watch the terrace fields will see full granaries).

CRITICAL APPARATUS AND MUSICAL NOTATION
Linguistic and metric classification of Tangkhul poetry (Ruivah, 1993):
- Hao La: Ancient festival chant; pentatonic scale, prolonged final syllables.
- Mawonzai: Romantic dialogue; antiphonal structure between male and female choruses.
- Kathi La: Dirge; minor tonal inflection, punctuated by ceremonial drum beats.
