CHAPTER 1: ORIGIN TRADITIONS AND MIGRATION FROM MAKHEL
Tangkhul oral literature unequivocally traces ancestral migrations back to Makhel in northern Manipur and contiguous Khezhakenoma in modern Phek district. The myth of the magic stone at Khezhakenoma where paddy dried in doubled measure is preserved across both Tangkhul and Tenyimia tribes.
From Makhel, three brothers departed: the eldest became the Meitei of the valley, the middle the Tangkhul of the eastern hills, and the youngest the Northern Naga groups.

--- Page 5 ---
CHAPTER 1 (Continued): SETTLEMENT OF THE UKHRUL RIDGE
Oral traditions account for the settlement of Hundung, Ukhrul (Hunphun), Choithar, Shirui, and Lunghar. Hundung and Hunphun represent the dual pivotal chieftaincies from which daughter settlements radiated throughout the Raphei (Northern), Rem (Western), Kharao (Southern), and Khanggo (Eastern) geographical divisions.
Toponyms such as Shirui Kashong (Shirui Peak) mark sacred boundary points in ancient territorial songs.

--- Page 6 ---
CHAPTER 1 (Continued): LINGUISTIC AND ETHNOGRAPHIC MAPPING
While standard Tangkhul is based on the dialect of Hunphun (Ukhrul), the tribe comprises over one hundred villages, each historically speaking a distinct localized vernacular (e.g. Champhung, Huishu, Phadang, Tolloi, Chingjaroi).
Mutual intelligibility was historically maintained through trade jargons, market visits to Imphal, and inter-village covenants (pukreila alliances).

CHAPTER 2: THE VILLAGE REPUBLIC (WUNGRAM KASHANG)
The traditional Tangkhul village was an autonomous republic with defined territorial borders, forest tracts, hunting reserves, and communal water sources. At the head of each village stood the Awunga (hereditary chief), descended from the founding clan (khaying-shang).
The Awunga was not an absolute monarch but the first among equals, governing in strict concert with the Hangva (Council of Elders representing all village clans).

--- Page 8 ---
CHAPTER 2 (Continued): THE HANGVA COUNCIL AND JUDICIAL PROCEDURE
The Hangva met at the Awunga's courtyard (awunga-sang) to adjudicate disputes, announce festival schedules, declare seasonal agricultural taboos (genna / khamthei), and decide matters of peace or war.
Decisions were reached through consensus (ngalung). Unanimous assent was sought, and contentious matters were submitted to solemn traditional ordeals (theishak) before the village assembly.

--- Page 9 ---
CHAPTER 2 (Continued): THE DORMITORY SYSTEM (LONGSHIM)
Integral to village administration was the Longshim (youth dormitory system). Boys entered the Mayar Longshim and girls the Ngala Longshim upon puberty. Here youth received military training, learned ancestral ballads, folklore, weaving, woodwork, and tribal etiquette. The Longshim served as the village militia and the guardian of moral and customary conventions.

CHAPTER 3: AGRICULTURAL REGIMES: LUINGAM AND SHILMIK
Tangkhul subsistence economy was anchored upon wet-rice terrace cultivation (Luingam) and rotational slash-and-burn swidden agriculture (Shilmik).
Engineering terraces along precipitous mountain slopes required sophisticated gravity-fed irrigation channels (kong-kuir) maintained collectively by irrigation syndicates (luira-ngashun).

--- Page 11 ---
CHAPTER 3 (Continued): AGRICULTURAL RITUALS AND SEED SOWING
The agricultural calendar opened with the Luira Phanit (seed-sowing festival) in February. No villager was permitted to sow seeds until the Awunga and the Luingarakpa (priest of the fields) performed the sacred first sowing ritual.
Harvest was celebrated with Chumpha (post-harvest thanksgiving in December), where newly garnered paddy was consecrated in the village granaries (raizan).

CHAPTER 4: CLAN KINSHIP (SHANG) AND ANCESTRAL LAND TENURE
Tangkhul society is strictly exogamous and patrilineal, organized into clans (shang) and sub-lineages (shimkhor). Marriage within the same clan was strictly prohibited and constituted the grave social offense of shiman-kaphung.
Land tenure was threefold: (1) Village communal land (ram-ngalei), (2) Clan land (shang-ngalei), and (3) Individual hereditary family land (khasan-ngalei).

--- Page 13 ---
CHAPTER 4 (Continued): INHERITANCE AND MALE PRIMOGENITURE
Under customary law, ancestral homesteads and terrace fields passed through male primogeniture (the eldest son inheriting the main hearth, with shares distributed to younger brothers upon marriage).
Women enjoyed usufructuary rights over certain garden plots and received livestock, necklaces (kachao), and brassware as marriage portions (ngalawui shiman).

--- Page 14 ---
CHAPTER 4 (Continued): INTER-VILLAGE COVENANTS (PUKREILA)
When two villages entered into formal treaties, high-status women born in one village and married into the other acted as Pukreila (peace ambassadors). The person of the Pukreila was sacrosanct; harming an ambassador was regarded as an unforgivable breach of tribal chivalry that would invoke divine retribution (khamatui).

CHAPTER 5: HEAD-TAKING (RAI) AND THE LOGIC OF ANCESTRAL WARFARE
Head-taking among the Tangkhul was bound to cosmological balance, fertility of crops, and personal valor. It was not indiscriminate slaughter but ritual warfare governed by strict customary boundaries.
Heads taken were brought to the village entrance, received with ritual chants, and hung at the sacred village tree (khor-thing) or deposited at the village skull depository.

--- Page 16 ---
CHAPTER 5 (Continued): PEACE RITUALS AND SPEAR BURIAL
Wars were concluded through the solemn ceremony of spear burial (kachu-kapam) and the consumption of ceremonial pork and rice-beer between combatant Awungas. Broken oaths brought calamity upon future generations, reinforcing the supreme sanctity of customary treaties.

--- Page 17 ---
CHAPTER 6: TRADITIONAL RELIGION: KAMEO AND VARIVARA
Tangkhul cosmology recognized a Supreme Creator God (Varivara) who dwelt in the highest heavens, benevolent and just. Beneath Varivara were regional nature spirits (Kameo) inhabiting sacred groves, rivers, cliffs, and peaks.
Propitiatory offerings were made by the village priest (Maiba) to appease spirits causing sickness or crop failure.

--- Page 18 ---
CHAPTER 6 (Continued): FEASTS OF MERIT (MARINGPHAT)
Wealthy individuals who accumulated surplus grain and cattle performed progressive Feasts of Merit (Maringphat). These feasts entailed feeding the entire village community and allied settlements for multiple days, erecting memorial megaliths (lung-thei), and carving house-fronts with horn-motifs (lengcheng-pakhang).

--- Page 19 ---
CHAPTER 7: THE COLONIAL ENCOUNTER AND CONTEMPORARY ERA
The arrival of British administration in the late 19th century and the arrival of Rev. William Pettigrew at Ukhrul in 1896 initiated dramatic socioeconomic transformations. Roman script literacy, modern medical care, and Christianity gradually replaced the head-taking regime.
Yet Tangkhul customary laws, land rights, and village identity endured, forming the foundation of modern Naga political consciousness and solidarity in northeast India.
